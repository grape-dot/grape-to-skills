## 1. Core Style Definition

An Eastern visual style merging the classical Chinese ink scroll with modern high-end magazine editorial design. The canvas is built on pale beige, warm ivory, and aged-paper parchment or rice-paper textures: natural fibers, subtle mottling, gentle wear, faint water stains, and a soft patina of age — while remaining clean, restrained, and refined, never aggressively distressed or artificially aged.

The visual language fuses Song-dynasty landscape painting, traditional gongbi bird-and-flower painting, antique bookbinding, scroll-unfolding structures, and Eastern negative-space aesthetics with Swiss International Typographic Style, modern art magazines, museum exhibition catalogs, and premium cultural publication editorial design. Traditional art elements are reorganized through modern graphic design, forming a visual system where classical atmosphere and contemporary order coexist.

Composition follows a clear modular grid, asymmetric balance, generous negative space, vertical columns, and horizontal information blocks. A stable visual axis anchors the layout, with dark vertical color blocks, narrow columns, numbered zones, image windows, and hairline dividers used locally, giving the page the sophistication of a magazine table of contents, a cultural feature spread, an exhibition catalog, or a brand style guide.

Traditional elements may include: layered distant mountains, mist, pines, pavilions, a lone boat on the river, flying birds, bamboo leaves, flowering branches, seals, scroll edges, classical motifs, pale-gold line work, and ink wash diffusion. All elements exist as atmosphere and visual language — never reproducing the specific text, headlines, paragraphs, or layout content of any reference image.

**One-line positioning**: not a copy of an antique painting, not a modern poster with decals — a museum-grade visual system that *re-choreographs classical atmosphere through modern editorial design*.

Avoid entirely: high saturation, neon colors, large pure-black areas, strong digital gradients.

## 3. Materials & Surface Texture

The paper carries real but restrained physical texture:

- Rice-paper fibers, parchment grain
- Subtle aged edges, faint water stains, soft creases
- Print ink penetration, natural ink wash diffusion
- Matte foil stamping or gold-dust particles, slight misregistration
- Antique printing combined with premium modern printing

Materials must be fine, realistic, and tactile. Avoid: coarse noise, heavy damage, excessive stains, burnt edges, cheap vintage filters.

## 4. Composition & Layout Rules

The frame follows a modern magazine editorial structure, not a traditional painting:

- Organize content with a rigorous grid system
- Keep generous negative space; never fill every area
- Build hierarchy with vertical color blocks, narrow columns, section numbers, hairlines, and image windows
- Concentrate the main visual in 1–2 zones; keep the rest quiet
- Traditional landscape and bird-and-flower elements may cross blocks, but must not break grid order
- Create a primary/secondary relationship across the frame; avoid perfect symmetry
- Hairline dividers should be light, thin, restrained — no heavy borders
- Numbers, section markers, seals, and small decorative text serve as visual symbols only, never as content reproduction
- Vary visual rhythm between dense and sparse: "negative space — focal point — negative space"

---

## 5. Rendering Traditional Art Elements

- Landscapes use light ink, blue-green coloring, and pale color washes; mountain layers are clear, distant peaks fainter and softer
- Mist and water keep large open areas
- Architecture is small and refined, serving as a visual anchor within the landscape
- Bird-and-flower subjects blend gongbi precision with xieyi looseness — detailed but never hyperrealistic
- Bamboo, flowering branches, pines, and birds appear as sparse accents
- Gold line work may trace cloud motifs, ripples, mountain contours, or edge ornaments
- Seals are small and natural; never stack them
- Traditional elements must not read as tourism posters, period-drama posters, or game concept art — aim instead for art catalogs, cultural publications, museum prints, and premium brand visuals

## 6. Light & Atmosphere

Use soft, even, diffused natural light or softbox studio light. The overall image is low contrast, low saturation, quiet, and warm. Paper edges, scroll structures, and subtle embossing may carry gentle shadows to enhance physical depth — but never hard shadows, dramatic spotlights, or cinematic light effects.

Atmosphere keywords: Eastern, serene, refined, rational, restrained, warm, poetic, past-and-present fusion, collectible, cultured, museum-grade, editorial, premium publication quality.

## 7. Typography & Text Zones

This prompt defines visual style only; it does not reproduce any text content from reference images.

If the image requires text zones:

- Use abstract placeholder text, unreadable typographic blocks, or at most a minimal generic headline
- Chinese headlines may use modern calligraphic, Song (serif), or high-contrast serif typefaces
- English auxiliary text may use fine serifs or Swiss sans-serifs
- Clear type-size hierarchy, generous letter-spacing, relaxed leading
- Avoid long readable Chinese passages to reduce garbled characters and wrong glyphs
- Final official text should be typeset later in design software

## 12. FAQ

**Q: Generated text always comes out garbled.**
A: The default recommendation is "generate no readable text" (see §10), with official text typeset later. If in-image text is required, allow only a few abstract numbers and section markers.

**Q: The image feels too busy or too dirty.**
A: Check two things: whether the composition negatives are complete (crowded elements, missing negative space), and whether the main background color covers 60–70% of the frame — state explicitly "large areas of negative space" and "background occupies most of the frame".

**Q: I want a different subject (e.g. jade, lacquerware).**
A: Follow the variation formula in §11 — swap the core medium term ("ink scroll" → "lacquer craft"), and replace materials, elements, and negatives with the corresponding craft vocabulary while keeping the editorial layout logic unchanged.

**Q: My model handles English better than Chinese.**
A: This document is the English version — use it directly.
